12  Health Summit

The OC Health Summit was held on October 28, 2025, with a record turnout of 140 attendees, including hospitals, healthcare providers, community-based organizations, and academia, to review the current state of health in OC. This year’s theme, “From Data to Decisions: Shaping Orange County’s Health Future,” emphasized turning data collection and reporting into actionable decisions that drive engagement, improvement, and awareness of health outcomes.

An overview of the most recently available data was provided to participants, covering:

  • 2025 Community Partner Survey Results and focus groups data overview
  • Community Health Survey results were given
  • Preliminary results from the 2025 Community Asset Survey
  • Hanlon Method overview
  • Data Summary across assessments

12.0.1 Keynote Presentation

A presentation by Dr. Marisa Donnelly, PhD (Your Local Epidemiologist), examined how to reimagine public health in Orange County by centering trust, clear communication, and genuine community engagement across the full cycle of assessment, prioritization, action, and feedback. She emphasized that public health is inherently local, seen in challenges such as vaccine hesitancy, maternal-health gaps, and overdose spikes.

Dr. Donnelly recommended strategies that included meeting residents where they are (leveraging social media more effectively and amplifying positive, human messages), moving beyond one-way information to two-way engagement (timely responses, listening for concerns and rumors, and inviting feedback), and treating trust as an ongoing practice by communicating early, often, and with acknowledged uncertainty. Dr. Donnelly described a transition from top-down, linear models to decentralized, community-anchored networks that rely on trusted messengers (e.g., teachers, faith leaders) and encouraged practitioners to “play offense” by anticipating narratives, learning from what worked and what didn’t, and forging new partnerships (e.g., collaborations between parent influencers and epidemiologists). Building on the prior CHIP focus areas of reducing food insecurity, increasing cancer screening, and decreasing opioid deaths. She challenged participants to bridge the gap between priorities and practice by elevating community-defined needs, discontinuing approaches that don’t serve residents, and strengthening health literacy and social media curricula so people can better discern credible information while providers focus on measurable outcomes, independent of political influence. Q&A highlighted algorithmic realities (content that spreads anger), the importance of staying audience-focused rather than partisan, the need for patient–clinician partnerships in an era of rapid information access, and emerging research efforts that integrate substance use, violence prevention, and HIV services to improve local impact.

12.0.2 Data Update

An Orange County Health Data Update was presented to participants, summarizing statewide changes and local indicators that will guide the next planning cycle. The New York State Prevention Agenda 2025–2030 elevates social determinants of health and extends the Community Health Improvement Plan (CHIP) to a six-year cycle, with at least one county priority required in the SDOH domain. Locally, regional assessments will post soon; the Orange County Community Health Assessment (CHA) is slated for completion by December, and the new six-year CHIP is due in June 2026. This work is informed by an expanded evidence base, including the 2024 Community Health Survey, Community Partner and Community Asset surveys, focus groups with underrepresented residents, and ongoing updates from the Health Equity Dashboard.

Key findings underscore the need for equity-focused, measurable strategies. In 2023, the leading causes of death were cardiovascular disease, cancer, and unintentional injury; nearly half of the deaths were premature, exceeding the statewide rate. Economic strain remains pronounced, with widening food insecurity among residents earning under $25,000, and many households spending at least half their income on housing. Cancer mortality is the highest in the Mid-Hudson region. It is the eighth worst in New York State, and about 20% above the state rate, while essential immunizations by age two trail regional and state (excluding NYC) benchmarks. Together, these results reinforce a CHIP that centers SDOH, pairs clinical and community strategies, and tracks progress over the six-year horizon.

12.0.3 Priority Area Voting

Summit participants were then invited to vote on the health indicators where they felt Orange County could make the greatest impact, using the results of the Hanlon prioritization as a guide. The following indicators were included:

  • Cancer
  • Childhood Immunizations
  • Childhood Obesity
  • Diabetes
  • Food Security
  • Infant Mortality
  • Opioid Burden
  • Physical Activity
  • Prenatal Care
  • Suicide

Each participant received three adhesive dots to place on the indicators they believed should be prioritized. This dot-voting process allowed partners to visually express their collective judgment and quickly identify areas of shared concern. When the votes were tallied, three indicators emerged as clear priorities: Food Security ranked first with 80 votes, Cancer ranked second with 42 votes, and Opioid Burden ranked third with 35 votes. These selections guided the subsequent focus of planning and discussion for the Community Health Improvement Plan.

12.0.4 Forces of Change Discussion

A Forces of Change Assessment (FOCA) discussion was held to better understand the trends, factors, and events that are shaping health in Orange County and influencing how effectively the local public health system can respond. Partners emphasized that virtually all of the county’s priority health issues are rooted in broader economic conditions. Participants noted that Orange County’s food system is heavily tied to federal policies and funding streams, including the USDA. Ongoing uncertainty around federal government operations and potential shutdowns was identified as a major threat to school meal programs, food pantries, and soup kitchens across New York State. These economic and policy forces were seen as foundational drivers behind food insecurity and many of the county’s other health priorities.

Access to prevention and treatment services emerged as another dominant theme. Partners highlighted the need to strengthen colorectal cancer screening and survivorship support for residents at high risk of recurrence. They also underscored the importance of coordination and patient follow-up, as well as education and advocacy, to ensure that individuals can move more seamlessly through the continuum of care. In the behavioral health space, participants described a critical gap in detoxification services, particularly on weekends, and called for opening and streamlining access so that people in crisis can enter care more fluidly. Stigma surrounding substance use disorder, mental health, suicide, and help-seeking was identified as a persistent barrier that discourages residents from reaching out for support.

Participants also noted that Orange County has a wide range of resources and programs that many residents simply do not know exist. Efforts such as Hope Not Handcuffs, Welcome Orange (with an upcoming orientation at the end of November), and various local coalitions were highlighted as important assets that could be leveraged more fully. Partners suggested designating staff or partners to proactively identify evidence-based resources and share them directly with clients, rather than waiting for people to seek them out. The underutilization of the 311 and 988 lines was cited as a missed opportunity: both numbers are “full of resources and ready to share,” and attendees emphasized that “every day a phone call saves someone’s life.”

Several forces of change were tied to specific populations and cross-jurisdictional issues. The political environment was recognized as a stressor that can negatively impact mental health and community well-being. Veterans were identified as a key group needing more focused outreach and engagement. Although many veterans are eligible for free health coverage, partners reported that some are reluctant to use services because they fear taking resources away from others. With more than 20,000 veterans in Orange County and only about 6,000 currently served, this was viewed as a significant gap and an opportunity to improve trust, communication, and navigation. Participants also noted substantial crossovers with neighboring Sullivan County, underscoring the importance of continued regional collaboration to address shared challenges and serve individuals who move across county lines.

Finally, partners stressed the importance of human connection as a protective factor in the face of these forces of change. Beyond programs and hotlines, residents often need someone who can “hold their hand or sit with them” in the hospital, in a nursing home, or at home during difficult times. The FOCA discussion reinforced that education, advocacy, coordination, and compassionate accompaniment, combined with stronger cross-sector networks, are essential to helping Orange County residents navigate complex systems, reduce the impact of economic and social stressors, and improve health across all priority areas.

12.0.5 Selected Priority Areas Discussion

After the FOCA, a focused discussion was held on the three health indicators identified as top priorities by summit participants: food security, cancer, and opioid burden.

For food security, partners in the network of hospitals and community-based efforts already have systems in place to support residents experiencing food insecurity. Examples included providing tailored bags of food for patients and families at discharge, accommodating dietary and clinical needs, and integrating safe sleep education and equipment into discharge planning for families with newborns. Community organizations and coalitions are working to ensure food reaches residents where they are, including those who are homebound or socially isolated, and there is an ongoing need for volunteers to assist with distribution and outreach. Participants also highlighted opportunities to connect food assistance with education on mental health and other supportive services.

In examining the cancer health indicator, participants emphasized prevention, early detection, and survivorship. Mobile screening efforts were identified as an important strategy to reach residents in their own communities, especially individuals facing transportation or access barriers. Alcohol-related cancers associated with binge drinking were raised as a concern, highlighting the need for integrated messaging around substance use and cancer risk. Rising colorectal cancer rates among younger adults, including women, were discussed, along with the importance of promoting earlier cancer screenings and increasing awareness of available health coverage. Participants also identified the need to expand survivorship supports and patient navigation to help residents move more effectively through screening, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up.

The opioid burden discussion centered on understanding root causes and strengthening the response. Participants raised questions about how broader substance use trends, including changing policies and norms, may be influencing opioid use in the community. There was broad agreement that additional resources must be directed toward mental health services, given that untreated mental health needs often underline substance use. Reducing stigma around opioid use was identified as essential, as stigma often prevents individuals and families from seeking help. Partners emphasized bringing education, screening, and support directly into the communities they serve, recognizing that taking the first step to get care is often the most difficult part of the recovery journey.

Taken together, these discussions around food security, cancer, and opioid burden underscored several cross-cutting themes: integrating clinical and community-based services, expanding outreach to underserved and isolated residents, reducing stigma, and strengthening navigation and follow-up across the continuum of care. Insights from this session will directly inform the development of goals, objectives, and strategies in the Community Health Improvement Plan, ensuring that Orange County’s efforts remain grounded in both data and community experience.
